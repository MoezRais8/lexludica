Modding is as old as PC gaming itself. From custom *Doom* levels to total conversions, players have always extended the works they love. Some of these projects became franchises in their own right: *Counter-Strike* began as a *Half-Life* mod, *Dota* as a custom *Warcraft III* map.

## A derivative work by nature

A mod modifies, extends or repurposes an existing game. Under French law it is a **composite work**: a new creation incorporating a pre-existing work without the participation of its author[^1]. The modder owns rights in their original contribution, but subject to the rights of the game's author[^2].

> The modder creates, but on someone else's ground.

Any adaptation made without authorisation is in principle unlawful[^3]. Legally, a mod exists only through the publisher's tolerance or licence.

## The licence as the real rulebook

This is why the actual law of modding is found in **end-user licence agreements**. Most publishers who ship creation tools allow free distribution of mods, prohibit commercial exploitation and sometimes reserve broad rights over community content.

## What remains unsettled

Can a community that creates value around a game claim a share of it? Copyright answers with individual ownership, while modding is collective and cumulative. It remains one of the most interesting open questions in video game law.

[^1]: `Art. L. 113-2 CPI` (French Intellectual Property Code), para. 2.
[^2]: `Art. L. 113-4 CPI`
[^3]: `Art. L. 122-4 CPI`
